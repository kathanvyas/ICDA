Chapter 01

Who are these patients?

Every patient in this study is a newborn baby with a serious congenital heart defect. The medical context comes first.

All 157 patients are newborn infants — most operated on within their first two weeks of life — born with a condition called

Single Ventricle Disease (SVD)
Single Ventricle Disease (SVD): A normal heart has four chambers: two upper chambers (atria) that receive blood, and two lower chambers (ventricles) that pump it. The right ventricle sends blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen; the left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood out to the entire body. In Single Ventricle Disease, one of these two ventricles is missing, severely underdeveloped, or so malformed it cannot work. The baby is left with one functional pump trying to do two jobs. Without surgery, this is incompatible with life.
.

Because of this anatomy, blood that has been to the lungs (oxygenated) and blood that has not (deoxygenated) mix together inside the single chamber. This is called

intracardiac mixing
Intracardiac mixing: In a healthy heart, oxygenated (bright red) and deoxygenated (dark red) blood are completely separated — two circuits running in parallel. In a single-ventricle heart, both streams enter the same chamber and blend. The resulting blood is only partially oxygenated, typically 75–85% saturated. This is why these babies look slightly bluish (called cyanosis) and why their SpO2 readings look "low" by adult standards but are actually acceptable for their condition.
. Their oxygen levels are intentionally lower than normal and must be carefully monitored.

All babies in this study had one of two surgeries to route blood to the lungs:

Procedure 1 — 51% of patients
Modified Blalock-Taussig-Thomas Shunt (mBTTS)
A small plastic tube surgically placed to connect one of the large arteries from the heart to the
pulmonary artery
Pulmonary artery: The large blood vessel that carries blood from the heart to the lungs. "Pulmonary" means relating to the lungs. In SVD this vessel is often not correctly connected to the single ventricle, so a shunt tube is placed to redirect blood there manually.
. Acts as a detour so blood can reach the lungs and pick up oxygen. Tube diameter: 3mm–5mm.
Procedure 2 — 49% of patients
Norwood Procedure
A more complex surgery that physically rebuilds the heart's outflow pathway. Used specifically for
HLHS
Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome (HLHS): "Hypoplastic" means underdeveloped. In HLHS, the entire left side of the heart — left ventricle, mitral valve, aortic valve, and aorta — is severely underdeveloped. The left ventricle is the body's main pump, so HLHS is one of the most serious congenital heart defects. These babies require the Norwood procedure within the first week of life. HLHS affected 29% of this cohort.
, the most severe form of SVD. Always requires
cardiopulmonary bypass
Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB): A machine that takes over the work of the heart and lungs during surgery. Surgeons connect tubes to the baby's major blood vessels; the machine pumps blood, removes carbon dioxide, and adds oxygen — keeping the baby alive while the heart is stopped. This allows work on a still, bloodless heart. CPB carries risks including inflammation, kidney stress, and brain effects. 100% of SH patients in this cohort had CPB.
.
The central clinical event
"Sudden hypoxemia" is a rapid, severe drop in blood oxygen caused by the shunt tube becoming blocked. When this happens, blood can no longer reach the lungs, oxygen levels plummet, and the baby faces an immediately life-threatening emergency. It happened in 14 of 157 babies (8.9%). Predicting it 30 minutes before it becomes critical is the entire goal of this research.

Sudden hypoxemia
Sudden hypoxemia: Hypoxemia = low oxygen in the blood (hypo = low; oxemia = oxygen in blood). "Sudden" distinguishes this from the chronically lower-but-stable oxygen levels these babies normally live with. A sudden drop is caused by an acute new problem — most often a blocked shunt. SpO2 may fall from ~80% to 60%, 30%, or near 0% within minutes.
is caused by
shunt occlusion
Shunt occlusion: Occlusion means blockage. The plastic shunt tube can become occluded by a blood clot (thrombus) forming inside it, by the tube kinking, or by external compression. Because the shunt is the only route for blood to reach the lungs in most of these babies, even partial blockage causes significant oxygen desaturation. Complete blockage is immediately life-threatening.
— the very tube placed to keep the baby alive begins to close off.

Chapter 02

The two datasets

Two completely different kinds of information were collected. Dataset 1 is the written medical chart; Dataset 2 is the ICU monitor's continuous recording.

Dataset 1 — Clinical features (all 157 patients)

A spreadsheet: one row per patient, 41+ columns. Each column is a fact about that baby — demographics, surgical details, postoperative complications, and most importantly whether they had sudden hypoxemia (coded 1) or not (coded 0). This is the target variable the models try to predict. Dataset 1 is what any doctor would see in a patient's chart.

Dataset 2 — Physiological signals (only the 14 SH patients)

Continuous time-series recordings from bedside ICU monitors: 51 signal channels at 2 samples per second, from surgery until discharge or event. This is the detailed numerical readout of every monitor in the ICU room, every half-second, over days or weeks. Critically, this data only exists for the 14 babies who had sudden hypoxemia — not the other 143. This limitation drives the need for synthetic data.

The 41 features in Dataset 1 fall into five groups:

1
Demographics and birth history
Age at surgery in days, weight at surgery (kg), birth weight,
gestational age
Gestational age: How many weeks of pregnancy were completed when the baby was born. Full-term = 40 weeks. Babies born before 37 weeks are premature. In this cohort, the median gestational age was 38 weeks. Premature babies have less developed organs and lower weight, adding complexity to surgery.
in weeks, sex, race, ethnicity, and whether premature. Age at surgery was the second most important CatBoost feature and a significant logistic regression predictor (p=0.028).
2
Cardiac anatomy
Classified into one of 8 categories using the
NPCQIC system
NPCQIC (National Pediatric Cardiology Quality Improvement Collaborative): A standardized coding system across US pediatric heart centers to categorize types of single-ventricle heart disease. HLHS = type 1, Unbalanced AVC = type 2, up to type 8. A shared coding system allows data from different hospitals to be compared and combined for research.
. Each anatomy type has a different risk profile and surgical approach.
3
Surgical parameters
Which procedure,
shunt size in mm
Shunt size: The diameter of the plastic tube placed during surgery in millimeters: 3.0mm, 3.5mm, 4.0mm, or 5.0mm. This was the single most important CatBoost feature (importance = 1.0). Key finding from the real data: not one baby with a shunt larger than 3.5mm experienced sudden hypoxemia. A larger shunt allows more blood flow and is less prone to critical narrowing.
,
shunt-to-weight ratio
Shunt-to-weight ratio: Shunt diameter (mm) divided by baby's weight at surgery (kg). Example: 3.5mm shunt in a 3.3kg baby = ratio of 1.06. A lower ratio means the shunt may be undersized for the body. This was the 4th most important CatBoost feature (importance = 0.57).
, whether CPB was used and for how long, whether
cross-clamp
Cross-clamp (XClamp): A metal clamp placed across the aorta during surgery to stop blood flow through it, giving the surgeon a still, bloodless operating field. Cross-clamp time is measured in minutes (typical range 79–109 min in this cohort). Longer cross-clamp time stresses the heart muscle due to interrupted blood supply.
was used, and whether
DHCA
Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA): The baby's body temperature is deliberately cooled to ~18°C (64°F). At this temperature the brain can survive without blood flow for up to 30–45 minutes — long enough for surgeons to complete the most delicate parts of the reconstruction. DHCA time in this cohort averaged 8–15 minutes for those who needed it.
was needed.
4
Comorbidities and syndromes
Whether the baby has an additional genetic syndrome (such as
DiGeorge syndrome
DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion): A genetic condition caused by a deletion on chromosome 22. In addition to heart defects, it causes immune system problems (thymus is often small or absent), calcium regulation issues, and can affect facial development. Babies with DiGeorge undergoing heart surgery face additional immune and metabolic challenges.
or
heterotaxy
Heterotaxy: From the Greek "different arrangement." The internal organs are not in their normal left-right positions. Heart, stomach, liver, and spleen may all be on the wrong side or in unusual positions. This often co-occurs with complex congenital heart disease and makes surgical planning much more difficult.
), chromosomal abnormalities, and whether
TAPVR
Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Return (TAPVR): Normally the four pulmonary veins (bringing oxygenated blood back from the lungs) drain into the left atrium. In TAPVR they connect to the wrong place. If a baby has both SVD and TAPVR, and both are repaired in the same surgery ("concurrent TAPVR repair"), the combined complexity creates dramatically higher risk: logistic regression OR = 20.45 (20× higher risk of sudden hypoxemia).
was present and repaired concurrently.
5
Postoperative complications
Low Cardiac Output Syndrome (LCOS)
LCOS: After heart surgery, the heart may be too weak to pump enough blood for the body's needs. Signs: low blood pressure, poor urine output, cool extremities, elevated lactic acid (a waste product when organs don't get enough oxygen). LCOS occurring before the SH event was one of the strongest predictors of sudden hypoxemia (p < 0.0001).
,
chylothorax
Chylothorax (ky-lo-THOR-ax): Collection of lymphatic fluid ("chyle" — milky white, fat-carrying) in the space around the lungs. Happens when the thoracic duct is accidentally damaged during surgery. The fluid compresses the lungs. In this study, chylothorax gave an OR of 12.35 in logistic regression — babies with it were 12× more likely to have sudden hypoxemia.
,
nitric oxide therapy
Nitric oxide (NO) therapy: A gas administered through the breathing tube that relaxes and dilates the blood vessels in the lungs, reducing pulmonary hypertension (high pressure in the lung blood vessels) which commonly occurs after open-heart surgery. In this cohort, 57% of SH patients received it vs only 19% of non-SH patients. It was the 3rd most important CatBoost feature.
, open sternum (chest bone left open due to heart swelling after surgery), arrhythmia, sepsis, and others. For SH patients, timing is also recorded: did each complication occur before or after the SH event?
Chapter 03

The physiological signals explained

51 signal channels, all recorded at 2 samples per second. Every major signal type in plain language.

SpO2 — Oxygen Saturation
Measured by a clip on the baby's finger, toe, or ear. A light shines through the skin; the device measures what percentage of the blood's
hemoglobin
Hemoglobin: A protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen. In the lungs it binds oxygen molecules; in body tissues it releases them. Fully loaded = bright red. Depleted = dark red. SpO2 measures the percentage currently carrying oxygen. SVD babies intentionally run at 75–85% due to blood mixing.
is carrying oxygen. SVD babies run at 75–85% — lower than healthy people (95–100%). When the shunt starts to block, SpO2 drops. Type A events: gradual decline over hours. Type B events: sudden crash to near zero.
HR — Heart Rate (beats per minute)
Measured continuously from chest electrodes (ECG leads). Normal newborn: 100–160 bpm. SVD babies often run slightly higher. When oxygen falls, the body compensates by pumping faster (
compensatory tachycardia
Tachycardia (tak-ih-KAR-dee-ah): Abnormally fast heart rate. In newborns, generally above 160–180 bpm. The heart beats faster to compensate for low oxygen — pushing more beats per minute even if each beat delivers less. This is an early warning sign before clinical deterioration becomes obvious.
). At the acute event, HR may spike to 200–250 bpm or suddenly collapse (
bradycardia
Bradycardia (brad-ih-KAR-dee-ah): Abnormally slow heart rate. In newborns, generally below 80–100 bpm. Bradycardia during a sudden hypoxemia event signals that the heart muscle itself is now oxygen-deprived and the compensatory mechanisms have failed — a cardiac emergency.
).
ABP — Arterial Blood Pressure (Systolic / Mean / Diastolic)
Measured continuously via an
arterial line
Arterial line (A-line): A thin catheter inserted directly into an artery (usually the radial artery in the wrist, or the umbilical artery in newborns). Because arteries are under higher pressure than veins, the arterial line gives a continuous real-time reading with every heartbeat. All critically ill post-surgical newborns in the ICU have one.
placed in an artery. Three values per beat: systolic (peak as heart contracts), diastolic (lowest as heart relaxes), and mean arterial pressure. Normal newborn: systolic ~65–80
mmHg
mmHg (millimeters of mercury): The universal unit for blood pressure. It refers to how high (in mm) a column of mercury would be pushed by that pressure. Blood pressure of 80/40 mmHg means systolic pressure can push mercury 80mm high, diastolic 40mm. The unit dates from when mercury manometers were used to measure blood pressure.
, mean ~45–55, diastolic ~30–45. During a Type B event, systolic can fall to 30–40 mmHg and diastolic to 5–10 mmHg.
CVP — Central Venous Pressure
Pressure in the large veins returning blood to the right side of the heart, measured via a
central venous catheter
Central venous catheter (CVC): A thin flexible tube inserted into a large central vein (internal jugular, subclavian, or femoral) and advanced until the tip sits just above the heart. It allows pressure measurement, medication delivery, and blood sampling. Almost all critically ill post-cardiac-surgery newborns have one.
. Normal: 6–12 mmHg. Rising CVP means blood is backing up in the veins — a sign the heart is struggling to pump it forward. CVP was observed to rise gradually in the hours before Type A sudden hypoxemia events.
NIRS RSO2 — Regional Oxygen Saturation (4 channels)
Pads on the forehead (Ch1, Ch2 = cerebral) and abdomen (Ch3, Ch4 = somatic/gut) use
near-infrared spectroscopy
Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS): Uses light at 700–1000 nm (invisible to human eyes) to penetrate several centimeters into tissue. Oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorb this light differently, so the device calculates the oxygen saturation within that tissue region. Unlike SpO2 (which measures circulating blood at a fingertip), NIRS shows whether the organs are actually receiving and using oxygen. It responds more slowly than SpO2 — typically with a 5-minute lag.
to measure local oxygen levels. Normal: 55–75%. Responds ~5 minutes after SpO2. In real patient recordings, cerebral channels 1 and 2 showed slightly different pre-event patterns, suggesting the two brain hemispheres may respond differently.
Resp Rate, CO2, O2 — Respiratory parameters
These babies are on a
mechanical ventilator
Mechanical ventilator: A machine that breathes for the patient by pushing air and oxygen into the lungs through a tube in the windpipe. After heart surgery, babies cannot breathe adequately on their own due to anesthesia and surgical stress. The ventilator controls rate, pressure, and oxygen percentage based on continuous monitoring.
after surgery. The ventilator measures: respiratory rate (30–60 breaths/min for a newborn),
end-tidal CO2
End-tidal CO2 (EtCO2): The concentration of CO2 at the end of each exhaled breath. CO2 is the waste gas produced when cells use oxygen. EtCO2 reflects how efficiently the lungs are clearing CO2. Normal: 35–45 mmHg. Low EtCO2 can indicate poor circulation; high EtCO2 suggests under-ventilation.
, and the oxygen fraction in inhaled and exhaled air. Rising respiratory rate was observed during the prodromal phase before some SH events.
ECG — ST segments and PVCs
The
electrocardiogram
Electrocardiogram (ECG/EKG): Records the heart's electrical impulses through 12 electrodes on the chest and limbs, showing electrical activity from 12 different angles. Each heartbeat waveform has named parts: P wave (atria contracting), QRS complex (ventricles contracting), and T wave (ventricles recovering). The ST segment — the flat stretch between QRS and T — should be electrically quiet; abnormalities indicate the heart muscle is under stress.
continuously monitors:
ST segment changes
ST segment changes: When heart muscle is not getting enough oxygen, abnormal electrical currents during the "quiet" ST period cause it to rise above or fall below the baseline. The Russin et al. feature engineering calculated both the mean ST deviation and its variability over 5-minute windows, across all 12 ECG leads.
(indicating heart muscle stress) and
PVCs
Premature Ventricular Contractions (PVCs): Extra heartbeats originating from the ventricles instead of from the normal heart pacemaker. They appear as wide, unusual-looking beats on the ECG. Many PVCs per minute indicate the heart muscle is irritable and stressed. In this study, PVC count rose during the prodromal window before some SH events, making it a useful early warning signal.
(extra beats indicating cardiac irritability). Both increased during the pre-event prodromal window in some patients.

backward stepwise elimination
Backward stepwise elimination: Start with all features included. Calculate a p-value for each. Remove the feature with the highest p-value (least statistically significant). Refit the model. Repeat until all remaining features have p-values below 0.05, meaning we're 95% confident the relationship is real rather than random chance.

Chapter 05

What the real signal recordings revealed

Plotting the actual ICU monitor data for the 14 SH patients showed two distinct event patterns.

Type A — Gradual (~40% of events)
Visible warning signals hours before crisis.
SpO2 shows increasing variability and slowly drifts down over 4–6 hours.
HR gradually climbs during the
prodromal window
Prodromal phase: The early warning period before a medical crisis. In Type A SH patients, a 2–8 hour window where SpO2 variability increases, HR rises gradually, and CVP rises.
Type B — Abrupt (~60% of events)
Near-normal signals right up to the event.
SpO2 appears stable, then crashes suddenly within minutes.
HR normal, then spikes to 200–250 bpm or suddenly collapses.

Observations from real recordings updated the synthetic generator: SpO2 baselines are higher than assumed (raising mean to 82%), most patients have only 1-2 days of pre-event signal data, and all patients show frequent brief SpO2 "dips" due to repositioning or apnea.

Chapter 06

Why synthetic data was needed

The research goal is to test a clinical dashboard with real healthcare workers. Three problems make using real data impossible.

1
Insufficient Variety
Only 14 SH patients have signal data — not enough for a robust user study with diverse scenarios and false alarms.
2
Privacy Barriers (HIPAA)
Showing real patient records to study participants without extensive de-identification and consent is restricted by
HIPAA
HIPAA: A US federal law strictly protecting patient health information. Synthetic data bypasses this barrier entirely.
.
3
No Ground Truth Answer Key
To score clinician performance, you need a known "correct" answer prepared in advance. Real clinical events are messy and ambiguous.

Building Dataset 2 — the ICU signals

Generating continuous physiological time-series for all 100 patients.

Step 1 — The AR(1) baseline model

ICU signals show

autocorrelation
Autocorrelation: Correlation of a signal with its past self. This ensures HR changes gradually over minutes rather than jumping randomly.
. We use an AR(1) process to model this smoothness.

X[t]  =  μ  +  φ · (X[t−1] − μ)  +  ε[t]
μ = long-run mean  •  φ = autocorrelation strength  •  ε[t] = small random noise

Step 2 — Baseline Modifiers

Dataset 1 features modify the signals: Heavier babies get higher blood pressure; HLHS babies run higher heart rates.

Step 3 — Deterioration Trajectory

SH patients follow a specific timeline: Stable baseline (1-3 days), Prodromal drift (varies by type), Acute event (~30 min), and Recovery (5-7 days).
