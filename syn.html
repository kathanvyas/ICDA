Section 01

Who are these patients and what is the medical problem?

Before anything about data, models, or generation — you need to understand the clinical situation. Every decision in this study traces back to the specific physiology of these babies.

All 157 patients are newborn infants, most less than two weeks old, who were born with a condition called

Single Ventricle Disease (SVD)Single Ventricle Disease (SVD): A normal heart has four chambers — two upper (atria) and two lower (ventricles). The ventricles are the main pumping chambers. The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs; the left ventricle pumps blood to the body. In SVD, one ventricle is either absent, underdeveloped, or so malformed it cannot function. This leaves the baby with one working pump trying to do two jobs. Without surgery, the condition is fatal within days to weeks.
. Because of this, oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix inside the heart — a situation called
intracardiac mixingIntracardiac mixing: In a healthy heart, the two blood streams — oxygenated (bright red, returning from lungs) and deoxygenated (dark red, returning from body) — are kept completely separated by the heart's internal wall (septum). In SVD, both streams enter the same ventricle and mix. The result: blood pumped out to the body is only partially oxygenated. This is why SVD babies look slightly bluish (called cyanosis) and why their oxygen levels naturally run lower than in healthy children.
. Their oxygen levels are intentionally allowed to stay around 75–85%, whereas a healthy person runs at 95–100%.

All patients in this study had one of two major surgeries to ensure blood could reach the lungs:

Surgery Type 1 — 51% of patients
Modified Blalock-Taussig-Thomas Shunt (mBTTS)
A small plastic tube is surgically placed between a large artery in the body and the
pulmonary arteryPulmonary artery: The blood vessel that carries blood from the heart to the lungs. "Pulmonary" means relating to the lungs. In a normal heart, the right ventricle pumps directly into the pulmonary artery. In SVD, the malformed heart cannot route blood there properly — the shunt creates a detour.
. The tube acts as a bypass route, redirecting blood to the lungs. Available in sizes 3.0mm, 3.5mm, 4.0mm, and 5.0mm diameter. This tube — specifically whether it stays open — is the center of this entire study.
Surgery Type 2 — 49% of patients
Norwood Procedure
A more complex surgery that physically reconstructs the heart's main outflow pathway. Used specifically for
HLHSHypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome (HLHS): "Hypoplastic" = underdeveloped. The entire left side of the heart — left ventricle, mitral valve, aortic valve, and aorta — is severely underdeveloped. The left ventricle is the main pump for the body, so HLHS is one of the most critical congenital heart defects. Babies with HLHS require the Norwood procedure within the first week of life and always require cardiopulmonary bypass (a machine that takes over for the heart and lungs during surgery).
(29% of patients) and always requires
cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB)Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB): A machine that completely takes over the function of the heart and lungs during surgery. The baby's blood is routed out through tubes into the machine, which oxygenates it, removes carbon dioxide, and pumps it back — keeping the baby alive while the heart is stopped. CPB allows surgeons to work on a still, bloodless heart but carries risks including inflammation, bleeding, and effects on the brain and kidneys.
.
The critical event
"Sudden hypoxemia" is what happens when the shunt tube becomes blocked — blood can no longer reach the lungs, oxygen in the blood falls rapidly, and the baby faces an immediately life-threatening emergency. This happened in 14 of 157 babies (8.9%). Predicting it 30 minutes before it becomes critical is the entire goal of this research.

Sudden hypoxemiaSudden hypoxemia: Hypoxemia = low oxygen in the blood (hypo = low; oxemia = oxygen in blood). "Sudden" distinguishes this from the chronically low-but-stable oxygen these babies normally have. A sudden drop is caused by a new acute problem — most commonly the shunt tube becoming blocked (occluded). The oxygen saturation (SpO2) may fall from a stable 80% to 60%, 30%, or even near 0% within minutes. Each percentage point drop represents a significant worsening of the baby's condition.
was the target variable in all machine learning models — label 1 means the event happened, 0 means it did not.

Section 03

What the machine learning models found

Two models were trained on Dataset 1 — logistic regression and CatBoost. Each discovered something different and complementary about what predicts sudden hypoxemia.

Model 1 — Logistic Regression (AUC = 0.74)

Logistic regression works like a weighted checklist. Each feature gets a coefficient — a positive number means "this increases risk," a negative number means "this decreases risk." The model used

backward stepwise eliminationBackward stepwise elimination: Start with all candidate features included in the model. Calculate the p-value for each. Remove the feature with the highest p-value (least statistically significant). Refit without it. Repeat until every remaining feature has p < 0.05. This ensures the final model contains only features where we are at least 95% confident the relationship is real, not coincidence.
to narrow down to the most significant predictors.

The

odds ratio (OR)Odds ratio (OR): How much more (or less) likely an outcome is for one group vs another. OR = 1.0 = no difference. OR = 2.0 = twice as likely. OR = 12.35 = 12× more likely. OR = 0.87 per day = each additional day reduces odds by 13% (protective). OR > 1 is a risk factor; OR < 1 is protective.
tells us the strength of each predictor. Here is the full result:

🔴 Important: why p=0.999 means "too strong" not "not significant"

When shunt size >3.5mm and CPB were included, logistic regression produced p=0.999 — which looks like "not significant." This is actually a mathematical breakdown called quasi-complete separation.

In the real data: not a single SH patient had a shunt larger than 3.5mm. And essentially all SH patients had CPB. When one group (SH=1) has 0% or 100% of a feature and the other group doesn't, the logistic regression algorithm tries to assign an infinite weight to that feature. The math cannot compute a meaningful coefficient — it overflows. The software produces nonsensical numbers.

Example: Imagine trying to guess which patients are SH just from shunt size. Every patient with a 4mm or 5mm shunt was NOT-SH (zero exceptions). Every patient with a 3.0mm or 3.5mm shunt might or might not be SH. The line between groups is so clean on the >3.5mm side that the model cannot quantify "how strong" — it just knows it's infinitely strong.

Correct interpretation: These features are among the most powerful predictors in the dataset. The p=0.999 is a sign of data perfection, not weakness. CatBoost handles this naturally with decision tree splits, which is why it outperforms logistic regression by 6 AUC points.

Model 2 — CatBoost (AUC = 0.80, best at 6 features)

CatBoostCatBoost: A gradient boosting algorithm that builds hundreds of decision trees sequentially — each tree learns from the mistakes of the ones before it. Unlike logistic regression (which can only find straight-line relationships), CatBoost finds complex nonlinear patterns and interactions between features. It handles categorical features (like shunt size class or anatomy type) particularly well. The name comes from "Categorical Boosting."
was tested with different subsets of features, from 3 up to 13. The model peaked at exactly 6 features with

AUC = 0.80 means: if you randomly pick one SH patient and one non-SH patient, there is an 80% chance the model gives the SH patient a higher risk score. The improvement from 0.74 (logistic regression) to 0.80 (CatBoost) is clinically meaningful — it correctly ranks 6% more patient pairs.
. Adding more features beyond 6 actually reduced performance because they added noise.

Example: Why CatBoost finds shunt size more powerfully than LR

CatBoost used a 3-class encoding of shunt size: class 0 (<3.5mm) = high risk, class 1 (=3.5mm) = intermediate, class 2 (>3.5mm) = strongly protective. A decision tree can ask "is shunt size class 2? → if yes, predict non-SH with high confidence." This nonlinear threshold rule is exactly what logistic regression cannot express — LR can only say "larger shunt = lower risk in a straight line," but the real data shows a threshold effect, not a gradient.

What both models agree on

Three features both models prioritize
Chylothorax, TAPVR concurrent repair, and Age at Surgery appear as significant in both models. This convergence across two different mathematical approaches is strong evidence these are genuine risk factors, not coincidences of the small SH sample size.
Why CatBoost scores 6 AUC points higher
Three reasons: (1) It handles quasi-complete separation naturally through tree splits. (2) It captures the nonlinear shunt size threshold. (3) It finds the interaction between nitric oxide + small shunt = very high risk, which logistic regression (which only looks at features individually) misses entirely.
Section 04

What the real signal recordings revealed

By examining the actual ICU monitor plots for several of the 14 SH patients in the hours around their events, five important observations shaped the signal generator design.

Finding 1 — Two completely different event types exist

Looking at plots for Patients 2, 3, 6, and 11, two distinct clinical pictures emerged that look almost nothing alike:

Type A — Gradual onset (40% of events)
Example: Patient 6. Signals begin drifting hours before the event.
SpO2 shows increasing variability and slow downward drift over 4–6 hours
Heart rate gradually climbs in what is called the
prodromal phaseProdromal phase: "Prodromal" comes from Greek meaning "running before." It describes early warning signs that precede a medical event — like the visual disturbances before a migraine. For Type A sudden hypoxemia, the prodromal phase is 2–8 hours of gradually worsening signals: SpO2 slowly drops, HR slowly rises, CVP slowly rises. These changes are subtle and may not trigger clinical alarms, but they are detectable using statistical methods.
CVP rises gradually in the hours before the event
SpO2
nadirNadir (NAY-deer): The lowest point reached by a measurement during an event. If SpO2 starts at 82% and falls to 58% at its worst, 58% is the nadir. The word comes from astronomy where it means the point directly below — opposite of "zenith." Used in medicine to emphasize the most dangerous moment of a deterioration episode.
: 50–65% — still dangerously low, but not near zero
HR peak: 165–180 bpm (baseline ~140)
A monitoring algorithm can detect this 30 min before it peaks
Type B — Abrupt onset (60% of events)
Examples: Patients 2, 3, 11. Near-normal signals until the moment it happens.
SpO2 appears stable, then crashes within minutes
Heart rate appears normal, then either spikes to 200–250 bpm or collapses toward zero
Virtually no prodromal phase (less than 30 minutes of warning)
SpO2 nadir: 5–25% — catastrophic, near-complete oxygen loss
Both systolic and diastolic BP collapse at the event (systolic to 30–40 mmHg, diastolic to 5–10 mmHg)
Essentially undetectable 30 min before it happens
Real example — Patient 11 (Type B)

The signal plot for Patient 11 shows HR running steadily around 145 bpm and SpO2 around 90% for the full 4 hours before the event. Then, within approximately 10 minutes of the SH timestamp, SpO2 drops from ~90% straight down to nearly 0%, and HR spikes violently before collapsing. There is essentially no gradual warning — this is a textbook Type B (abrupt) event. A monitoring algorithm using the 30-minute pre-event window would see z-scores near zero in this patient — which is correct by design.

Finding 2 — SpO2 baselines are higher than assumed

We originally assumed SVD babies run at a mean SpO2 of 79%. The real plots showed Patients 6 and 11 had baselines of 85–95%. The reason: many of these babies are on supplemental oxygen or

nitric oxide therapySee Section 2 definition above.
, which pushes their saturation higher than the typical mixing baseline. This observation directly changed a parameter in the generator.

Observation
SpO2 baseline too low in original generator
Original mean = 79%, σ = 2.5%. Real data shows mean closer to 82–86% in patients on oxygen support or nitric oxide.
→
Change made: Raised SpO2 baseline to mean 82%, σ = 4.0%. Added nitric oxide modifier: patients with NitricOxide=1 get an additional +4% offset on their SpO2 baseline.

Finding 3 — Most SH patients had only 1–2 days of data before their event

A bar chart in the study presentation (Days Analysis slide) showed how much signal data existed before and after the SH event for all 14 patients. The observation was striking: most patients had only 1–2 days of usable pre-event data. Patient 1 had just 1.5 days. Patients 6–14 had all ≤ 2 days before their event.

Observation
Original generator used 14 days of pre-event stable baseline — wrong
We originally generated 14+ days of stable signal before the SH event. Real data shows most SH events happen within the first 1–3 days post-surgery. A 14-day stable window before the event is unrealistic.
→
Change made: SH patients now get 1–3 days of pre-event stable baseline (sampled from a log-normal with median ~1.8 days), the SH event, then a 5–7 day recovery phase. Total LOS for SH patients: typically 7–11 days.

Finding 4 — All patients show frequent brief SpO2 dips

Looking at non-SH patients' signal records, SpO2 was not a smooth flat line. It regularly dipped by 5–15 percentage points for 1–5 minutes, then recovered. These transient dips happen from repositioning the baby,

brief apnea episodesApnea: A pause in breathing. In newborns, especially after heart surgery, brief apneic episodes (10–30 seconds) are common due to the effects of anesthesia, medication, or immature respiratory control. They cause temporary SpO2 drops that look alarming on the monitor but resolve quickly without intervention.
, probe displacement, or coughing.

Observation
Smooth AR(1) baseline is not realistic — all real patients have transient dips
If the synthetic non-SH patient SpO2 signal is too smooth, a monitoring algorithm would learn to treat ANY downward movement as a certain SH event. Real ICU data is noisy with random brief dips.
→
Change made: All 100 synthetic patients (SH and non-SH) now receive 3–10 randomly placed transient SpO2 dips per stay, each 5–15 percentage points deep and 1–5 minutes long. This makes the data realistic and prevents overfitting.

Finding 5 — The Russin feature engineering framework defines what "detectable" means

The signal data was processed using a specific approach from Craig Russin et al.: each raw signal is converted into 5-minute rolling averages, then

z-scoredZ-score (Russin normalization): Measures how unusual a current value is compared to recent history. Formula: Z = (current 5-minute average − mean of previous 24 hours) ÷ standard deviation of previous 24 hours. Z = 0 means "exactly average." Z = 1.5 means "1.5 standard deviations above average — somewhat unusual." Z = 3.0 means "very unusual." By normalizing each patient against their own 24-hour baseline, the model doesn't need to know what each patient's "normal" is in absolute terms — it detects deviations from their personal norm. This is important because different SVD babies run at different baseline HR and SpO2 values.
against the previous 24 hours. A z-score ≥ 1.5 standard deviations in the 30-minute pre-event window is considered a detectable early warning. The 30-minute prediction horizon means: can the model flag this event 30 minutes before it becomes clinically obvious?

Observation
Synthetic signals must produce z-scores ≥ 1.5 in Type A patients' 30-min pre-event window
Type A patients have a visible prodromal drift. Type B patients should NOT show z-scores above 1.5 — their events are near-undetectable. Both patterns needed to be validated in the synthetic data.
→
Change made: Added a Russin z-score validator that automatically checks all SH patients after generation. Type A pass rate: 75% ✓. Type B pass rate: 0% ✓ (correct by design — abrupt events are clinically undetectable).
Section 05

Why synthetic data was needed

The goal of this research is to build and test a clinical dashboard. Three concrete problems make testing with real data impossible.

Problem 1
Only 14 SH patients have signal data
A human factors user study needs diverse scenarios: different anatomies, different event types, false alarms, textbook cases, subtle cases. With only 14 SH patients — and zero signal data for the other 143 — there is no material to build a meaningful 10-scenario test battery.
Problem 2
HIPAA + IRB block real patient data in testing
HIPAAHIPAA: The US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Protects patient health information. Showing real patient records to study participants (the clinicians being tested) requires extensive de-identification, separate consent from patients' families, and IRB approval — adding months of regulatory delay.
regulations mean real patient data cannot be shown to study participants without extensive legal processes. Synthetic data contains no real patient information and bypasses this entirely.
Problem 3
No controlled "answer key" for evaluating clinicians
To evaluate whether a clinician correctly detected an event, you need a pre-defined correct answer. Real clinical outcomes are messy and ambiguous. Synthetic data gives you a controlled ground truth — you know exactly which patient is SH, exactly when the event occurs, and exactly what type it is.

Validation target: A CatBoost model retrained on the synthetic data should achieve AUC within ±0.03 of the real model's AUC. Our generator achieved AUC = 0.803 on the 6-feature model, compared to the real AUC of 0.7969 — a difference of only 0.006, well within the acceptable range.

Section 06

How Dataset 1 was built — step by step

Every design decision traces directly to a ML finding or real cohort statistic. Here is the complete process, with the reasoning behind each choice.

1
Assign the outcome label first — exactly 10 patients get SH=1

This is the very first operation. Before any other feature is generated, exactly 10 of the 100 patients are randomly selected to receive Sudden_Hypoxemia = 1. The remaining 90 get 0.

Why first? Every other feature will be drawn from a different probability distribution depending on whether the patient is SH or not. If we assign the label after generating features, the correlations won't be right. By labeling first, each feature is generated conditional on the outcome — which matches how the real data looks.

Why 10 (not 8 or 14)? The real rate was 8.9% (14 of 157). We rounded up slightly to 10% to ensure there are enough SH cases for diverse scenario selection. A rate of 10% closely mirrors the real clinical incidence.

Example

After step 1, the dataset might look like: Patient 5009 = SH=1, Patient 5010 = SH=1, Patient 5042 = SH=1... and 90 others = SH=0. Every feature generated afterward will "know" which group each patient belongs to.

2
Assign SH phenotype A or B

Each of the 10 SH patients is randomly assigned SH_Phenotype = "A" (40%) or "B" (60%), based on what we observed in the real signal plots. Non-SH patients get "none".

This phenotype assignment is stored in the Dataset 1 spreadsheet and is later read by the signal generator to determine what kind of deterioration trajectory to inject.

Example

In our generated dataset: Patient 5042 = Type A (gradual onset, detectable prodrome). Patient 5009 = Type B (abrupt onset, near-undetectable). Their signal files will look completely different around the event timestamp.

3
Shunt size — hard constraint from CatBoost rank-1 and LR quasi-separation

This is the single most important constraint in the entire generator. In the real data, not one SH patient had a shunt larger than 3.5mm. This produced quasi-complete separation in logistic regression and the highest importance score in CatBoost (1.0).

The code:

# SH patients: NEVER get shunt > 3.5mm (hard rule from real data)
def _shunt(is_sh):
    if is_sh:
        return rng.choice([3.0, 3.5], p=[0.07, 0.93])
    else:
        return rng.choice([3.0, 3.5, 4.0, 5.0],

6
Clinical constraint checker — fixing medically impossible combinations

After all features are sampled, an automatic rule-checker runs and corrects any violations. Five rules are enforced:

Rule 1: Norwood → CPB=1 always
The Norwood procedure physically requires the bypass machine. If a Norwood patient somehow got CPB=0, it is corrected to CPB=1.
Rule 2: SH → CPB=1 always
Every real SH patient had CPB. This is a hard rule — no SH patient in the synthetic data can have CPB=0.
Rule 3: CPB=0 → times all NaN
If bypass wasn't used, then CPB time, cross-clamp time, and DHCA time must all be blank. Can't have a CPB duration when there was no CPB.
Rule 4: SH → no cardiac arrest
In the real data, no SH patient had a cardiac arrest. If an SH patient was randomly assigned CompCardArrest=1, it is corrected to 0.
Rule 5: SH → shunt ≤ 3.5mm
Final safety net: if any SH patient ended up with shunt > 3.5mm after sampling, it is corrected to 3.5mm. (This should never happen due to Step 3, but the checker catches edge cases.)
Section 07

How Dataset 2 was built — generating the ICU signals

Unlike Dataset 1 (one row per patient), Dataset 2 is a continuous time-series for each patient. Generating it requires both statistical methods and physiological knowledge.

Step 1 — The baseline signal model: AR(1)

A real physiological signal does not jump randomly between readings. If heart rate is 140 bpm right now, it will be close to 140 bpm in 2 seconds. This property — called

autocorrelationAutocorrelation: Correlation of a signal with its own past values. A coin flip has zero autocorrelation — each flip is independent. Temperature has very high autocorrelation — today's temperature is strongly related to yesterday's. ICU physiological signals have moderate to high autocorrelation: HR drifts slowly over minutes, temperature over hours. The AR(1) coefficient φ (phi) controls this: φ = 0.99 = very smooth (temperature-like), φ = 0.80 = more variable (PVC count-like).
— is modeled using an AR(1) process (first-order autoregressive model):

AR(1) — First-Order Autoregressive Model
X[t] = μ + φ · (X[t−1] − μ) + ε[t]
X[t]The signal value at time t (e.g., heart rate right now)
μ (mu)The long-run baseline mean (e.g., 140 bpm for HR)
φ (phi)Autocorrelation: how much the current value depends on the previous one. Range: 0 to 1. Set to 0.96–0.97 for ABP and HR; 0.99 for temperature (very slow-changing).
ε[t]Random noise at this timestep. Drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation = σ × √(1−φ²)
Example: generating 10 steps of HR for a patient

μ = 140 bpm, φ = 0.96, σ = 8.0. Start at 140. Step 1: add noise → 141.2. Step 2: pull back slightly toward 140, add noise → 140.8. Step 3: → 142.1. Each step is strongly influenced by the previous value (φ=0.96) but with small random perturbations. The result looks like a real HR trace: smooth drift with physiological variability.

This is computed efficiently using scipy.signal.lfilter which processes all N timesteps in a single vectorized operation — no Python loop needed. This keeps the full 100-patient run under 35 seconds.

For the 10 SH patients, a deterioration script is injected on top of the baseline AR(1) signal. The structure of the timeline:

Stable baseline1–3 days
Prodromal driftType A: 2–8h · Type B: <30 min
Acute event~30 min
Recovery phase5–7 days

The delta (change) injected on each channel depends on phenotype. These are additive — they are added on top of the AR(1) baseline signal, so the result still looks like a physiological waveform, just drifting in the right direction:

Type A — injected changes (gradual)
SpO2 prodromal drop: −7 to −9 percentage points, linearly over 2–8 hours
SpO2 nadir: additional −18 to −24 points at the acute event (total drop from baseline: 25–33 pts)
HR prodromal rise: +20 to +30 bpm linearly over the prodromal window
HR at event: additional +30 to +40 bpm spike
CVP: +3 to +5 mmHg rise during prodromal window
Resp rate: +12 breaths/min rising through prodrome
PVC count: +3 to +8 extra beats/min in prodrome
NIRS Ch1: follows SpO2 × 0.60 with 5-min lag
NIRS Ch2: same but × 0.82 (slight divergence from Ch1)
Type B — injected changes (abrupt)
SpO2 prodromal: only −0.5 to −2 pts (essentially flat until the event)
SpO2 at event: −52 to −65 pts in ~30 minutes (nadir 5–25%)
HR prodromal: only +3 to +10 bpm (barely noticeable above noise)
HR at event: spike of +55 to +85 bpm (reaching 195–225 bpm)
ABP systolic: falls by –38 to –52 mmHg (to 30–40 mmHg floor)
ABP diastolic: falls by –30 to –45 mmHg (to 5–10 mmHg floor)
CVP, Resp, PVC: minimal change pre-event
Result: z-scores near zero in 30-min pre-event window — clinically undetectable ✓
# How the ramp is applied (simplified)
# "ramp_min" creates a linear slope from v0 to v1 between minutes s and e

if phenotype == "A":
    spo2_prod_drop  = rng.uniform(-9, -7)    # e.g. –8.2 pts over prodrome
    spo2_acute_drop = rng.uniform(-24, -18)  # e.g. –21 pts at event
else:  # Type B
    spo2_prod_drop  = rng.uniform(-2, -0.5)  # e.g. –1.1 pts (flat)
    spo2_acute_drop = rng.uniform(-65, -52)  # e.g. –58 pts (crash)

delta_spo2 = (
    ramp_min(n_trend, prod_start, sh_min, 0, spo2_prod_drop) +
    ramp_min(n_trend, sh_min, acute_end, 0, spo2_acute_drop)
)
# Add this delta on top of the AR(1) baseline:
spo2_signal = (spo2_baseline + delta_spo2).clip(50, 100)

Step 4 — Transient SpO2 dips for all patients

After the baseline and any SH trajectory are generated, all 100 patients (SH and non-SH alike) receive random brief SpO2 dips:

def transient_dips(arr, n_trend, rng, lo=50):
    n = int(rng.integers(3, 11))  # 3–10 dips per stay
    for _ in range(n):
        start    = int(rng.integers(60, n_trend - 60))
        duration = int(rng.integers(1, 6))      # 1–5 minutes long
        magnitude = float(rng.uniform(5, 15))  # 5–15 percentage points deep
        # Shape: ramp down then ramp back up (V shape)
        arr[start:start+duration] -= magnitude

Step 5 — Tiered storage architecture (solving the file size problem)

At 2 samples/second × 51 channels × 100 patients × 14–30 days = approximately 46 gigabytes uncompressed. We solved this with a two-tier approach:

Tier 1 — trend_1min
1 sample per minute, full stay
All 51 channels downsampled to 1 reading per minute for the entire LOS (14–30 days). Used for long-term trend viewing in the dashboard. Stored as float16 (half precision) with gzip compression. File size: ~3–5 MB per patient.
Tier 2 — burst_2hz
2 samples/second, ±6 hour window
The 23 core hemodynamic channels (HR, ABP, SpO2, CVP, NBP) at full 2 Hz resolution, but only for the ±6 hour window around the SH event (or mid-stay for non-SH patients). Used for alarm analysis and event detection. This is where the "2 Hz" requirement is preserved.

Both tiers are stored in an

HDF5 fileHDF5 (Hierarchical Data Format version 5): A binary file format designed for large scientific datasets. Think of it as a ZIP file that also acts as a database — it can store many arrays of data with names, compressed, all inside one file. Supports partial reading (read just the SpO2 channel without loading everything), compression (files 10–180× smaller than raw), and metadata storage (the patient's clinical features stored alongside the signals as "attributes"). Standard format in neuroscience, genomics, and clinical data science.
with gzip compression — one file per patient. Total: 433 MB for all 100 patients.

If Type B patients were showing high z-scores, it would mean the synthetic data was unrealistically easy to predict — a false validation pass.
